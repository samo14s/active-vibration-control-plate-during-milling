\title{\textbf{Position-scheduled LQG control with a spindle-synchronized
harmonic internal model for active vibration suppression in thin-walled
plate milling}}

\author{Sami Soufi$^{a,}$\thanks{Corresponding author.}\\[2pt]
\small $^a$National Engineering School of Sfax (ENIS), University of Sfax, Tunisia}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Milling of thin-walled workpieces is limited by forced vibration and
regenerative chatter of the compliant wall, whose dynamics as seen from the
moving cutter--workpiece engagement point vary continuously along the tool
path while the sensor and the piezoelectric patch actuator remain fixed.
The state of the art in workpiece-side active control either freezes the
design at one (worst-case) configuration or schedules simple
proportional--derivative gains heuristically, without state estimation,
optimality, or a stability argument.  This paper develops a
position-scheduled harmonic linear--quadratic--Gaussian (PSH-LQG)
controller that addresses the time-space variance structurally.  The key
observation is that the plate--actuator--sensor plant is time invariant:
what varies with the cutter position are the disturbance input map and the
performance output map.  Accordingly, a Kalman predictor with an internal
model of the periodic milling force---phasor states at the tooth-passing
frequency and its harmonics, coupled through an exact sinusoid
discretization---estimates the modal state and the harmonic force content
from a single fixed accelerometer, while a position-scheduled LQR weights
the deflection at the moving cutting point and a position-scheduled
regularized model inversion cancels the estimated harmonics there.
Actuator saturation, one-sample computation delay, and the analog
anti-aliasing chain are included in the design model; spillover is bounded
by a verification-driven per-harmonic gain mask against the full-order
model.  A broadband random-walk force state complements the harmonic model where
amplitude-bounded chatter concentrates sub-harmonic content below the
first plate mode.  On a finite-element benchmark reproducing a published
experimental plate-milling configuration---evaluated on a 20-mode truth
model with the nonlinear regenerative force loop closed, including the
multi-delay surface-memory mechanism that bounds supercritical
chatter---the proposed controller reduces the vibration RMS at the
moving cutting point by a factor of \RedPSH\ over the pass (versus
\RedCPD\ for an optimally tuned constant PD and \RedVPD\ for the
published time-space varying PD given the same tuning budget),
approaching an order of magnitude at the most compliant path region,
raises the critical depth of cut predicted from the closed-loop
frequency functions by \AcritMid\ at mid-path and \AcritEnd\ at the
path ends, and retains its performance
under $\pm 3\%$ modal-frequency error, $\pm 30\%$ damping error,
$\pm 20\%$ force-coefficient error, cutter runout, doubled computation
delay, and in-process material removal.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} thin-walled milling; active vibration control;
piezoelectric actuator; gain scheduling; Kalman filter; internal model
principle; regenerative chatter

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Thin-walled components dominate aerospace structures, and their finish
milling is routinely limited by vibration of the compliant workpiece
rather than of the machine: forced vibration at the tooth-passing
frequency (TPF) and its harmonics degrades the machined surface, and the
regenerative feedback between the wall deflection and the chip thickness
can escalate into chatter \citep{Altintas2012,Sun2025Review}.  Passive
remedies (added mass, damping layers, tuned absorbers) are
configuration-specific, while parameter selection from stability lobe
diagrams \citep{AltintasBudak1995} is fragile precisely for thin walls,
whose point dynamics change with every pass.  Workpiece-side \emph{active}
control with surface-bonded piezoelectric patches is therefore attracting
sustained attention \citep{Zhang2019,Wan2020,LiJMPT2021,DuJMP2022},
recently including learning-based controllers \citep{Nasiri2025} whose
very existence signals the community's dissatisfaction with fixed-gain
designs---though without the stability certificates that a
model-based synthesis can offer.

The specific difficulty of the thin-wall problem is its
\emph{time-space variance}: the cutter--workpiece engagement point travels
along the wall, so the transfer behaviour from the cutting force to the
machined point, and from the fixed actuator and sensor to that point, vary
continuously during the pass, and the wall itself loses material.
Wang \emph{et al.}\ \citep{WangSongLiu2019} identified this issue for a
cantilever plate instrumented with one accelerometer and one piezoelectric
patch, and proposed a time-space varying PD law (VPD) whose proportional
gain follows the cutter position through a quintic polynomial fitted to
31 point-wise tuned values.  This remains, to the best of the author's
knowledge (based on the literature search documented in the
accompanying repository), the only published controller scheduled on
the in-cut tool position; yet it is heuristic on every level: the gains are tuned by
repeated simulation, there is no state estimation and no optimality
notion, the schedule is smoothed without re-verifying stability, and the
periodic structure of the milling force is not exploited.

The broader literature brackets, but does not fill, this gap.  Fixed-gain
optimal and robust designs---LQG \citep{Parus2013,DuIJMS2022}, sliding
mode \citep{Wan2020}, LMI-based $H_\infty$ \citep{LiJMPT2021}, modal
control at the worst-case position \citep{DuJMP2022}, combined time-delay
control \citep{DuIJMS2024}---treat the plant as time invariant, accepting
the conservatism that Du \emph{et al.}\ acknowledge explicitly.  LPV
machining controllers exist for turning, scheduled on the setup-dependent
boring-bar overhang \citep{CEP2025LPV}, and LPV thin-wall \emph{models}
including material removal are established
\citep{Maslo2020,TuysuzAltintas2017,Shi2023}, but have not been closed in
a loop scheduled on the moving cut.  On the disturbance side, the
periodicity of the milling force at the spindle-synchronous TPF invites
internal-model action \citep{FrancisWonham1976,Johnson1971}; existing
machining implementations, however, are adaptive \emph{feedforward}
filters without stability certificates \citep{Wang2022FFT}, open-loop
excitation at tooth-passing-related frequencies \citep{Sallese2017}, or
comb filters that deliberately \emph{exclude} the harmonics from the
feedback loop \citep{LiMSSP2021}.  Outside machining, LPV resonant
observers for periodic disturbances with varying frequency are known
\citep{Ramos2015,Kinney2011,Bodson1997}, but on LTI plants without a
regenerative loop, input saturation, or a moving performance point.

\paragraph{Contributions.}  This paper closes the gap with a
position-scheduled harmonic LQG (PSH-LQG) architecture and a
correspondingly rigorous evaluation:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Structural treatment of time-space variance.}  We show that
for a plate milled along a straight path the plant
(voltage$\,\to\,$accelerometer) is LTI; the cutter position enters only
through the disturbance input map and the performance output map.  This
turns the heuristic gain-scheduling problem of \citep{WangSongLiu2019}
into a well-posed time-varying-weight LQG problem solved by Riccati
equations on a position grid.
\item \emph{Adaptive harmonic internal model with exact discretization.}
The periodic milling force is modelled by phasor states at the TPF
harmonics (synchronized to the spindle encoder), estimated by a Kalman
predictor whose coupling matrices are discretized exactly for sinusoidal
inter-sample behaviour, and cancelled at the \emph{moving} cutting point
through a position-scheduled, Tikhonov-regularized model inversion with a
leakage factor that trades a bounded ${\sim}1\%$ cancellation bias for a
guaranteed contraction budget against model mismatch.
\item \emph{Implementation realism and spillover control.}  Actuator
saturation with anti-windup (the predictor is driven by the saturated
input), one-sample computation delay, and the analog anti-aliasing
filter are part of the design model; the composite plant (modes + analog
filter) is discretized jointly so that above-Nyquist modes alias exactly
as in the sampled hardware.  A verification-driven per-harmonic gain mask
against the full-order model bounds observation/control spillover
\citep{Balas1978}.
\item \emph{Honest baselines and evaluation.}  The published VPD strategy
is re-derived with the same tuning budget as the proposed method
(per-position optimization on the true force spectrum, quintic
parameterization per \citep{WangSongLiu2019}), and we expose a genuine
hazard of that parameterization: the quintic fit can bridge over local
dips of the point-wise stabilizing gains and destabilize the loop, which
we repair by post-fit verification.  All controllers are evaluated on a
20-mode truth model with the nonlinear regenerative force loop (tooth
jump-out included), measurement noise, saturation and delay---a stricter
test than the design-model simulations common in the literature.
\end{enumerate}

The study is simulation-based by design, but it is anchored to a published
experimental configuration \citep{WangSongLiu2019}: geometry, materials,
sensor/actuator placement, drive-chain gains and saturation limits,
sampling rate, and the actuator authority (calibrated to the measured
static deflections) are taken from that work, and the observed
phenomenology---position-dependent vibration severity and near-resonant
harmonic amplification---is reproduced.  Section~\ref{sec:model} derives
the model; Section~\ref{sec:control} develops the controller and its
stability analysis; Section~\ref{sec:baselines} specifies the baselines;
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the campaign; Section~\ref{sec:concl}
concludes.

\section{Modelling of the plate--actuator milling system}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Plate and piezoelectric actuator}
\label{sec:plate}

The workpiece is a thin cantilever plate (Al--Mg alloy 5052,
$L\times W\times h_p = \SI{120}{mm}\times\SI{120}{mm}\times\SI{4}{mm}$,
clamped along $y=0$) with a piezoelectric patch
($[u_1,u_2]\times[v_1,v_2] =
[50,70]\times[20,58]\,\si{mm}$, $h_a=\SI{0.4}{mm}$) bonded on one face
and an accelerometer at $(x_s,y_s)=(60,100)\,\si{mm}$
\citep{WangSongLiu2019}.  Under Kirchhoff assumptions with the patch
stiffness and inertia neglected, Hamilton's principle yields
\begin{equation}
D\,\nabla^4 w + \rho h_p \ddot w
 = f(x,y,t)
 - q\,V(t)\left[\Delta\delta'_u(x)\,\Delta H_v(y)
 + \Delta H_u(x)\,\Delta\delta'_v(y)\right],
\label{eq:pde}
\end{equation}
where $D = E h_p^3/12(1-\nu^2)$, $\Delta H$ and $\Delta\delta'$ are
differences of Heaviside and Dirac-derivative distributions locating the
patch edges, and the electromechanical constant follows the
Crawley--de Luis thin-patch result \citep{CrawleyDeLuis1987,Preumont2011}
\begin{equation}
q=\kappa_a\,\frac{E_p d_{31}(h_p+h_a)}{2(1-\nu_p)} ,
\label{eq:q}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the energized patch applies the uniform biaxial bending moment
$m_p = qV$ per unit length along its boundary.  The dimensionless
authority factor $\kappa_a$ absorbs bonding-layer and effective-constant
uncertainty; it is identified below from the static deflections measured
in \citep{WangSongLiu2019}.

The saturation limits of the drive chain are treated as hard
constraints throughout, with anti-windup by construction (the estimator
is driven by the saturated command), in line with recent treatments of
actuator limits in active damping \citep{Bayoumi2024Saturation}.
Equation~\eqref{eq:pde} is discretized with the 12-DOF nonconforming ACM
rectangular element (nodal DOFs $w, w_{,x}, w_{,y}$) on a
$60\times60$ mesh chosen so the patch edges coincide with mesh lines.
Integrating the distributional actuation term by parts twice gives the
consistent actuation vector
\begin{equation}
B_{a,i} = -q \iint_{\text{patch}}
\left(\psi_{i,xx}+\psi_{i,yy}\right)\mathrm{d}A ,
\label{eq:Ba}
\end{equation}
evaluated by Gauss quadrature per element, so that
$\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf u} + \mathbf K\mathbf u = \mathbf F(t) +
\mathbf B_a V(t)$.
The first computed natural frequencies (239.6, 579.6, 1461.6, 1874.8,
2118.1~Hz) agree with Leissa's cantilever-plate coefficients
\citep{Leissa1969} to within 1\% (Table~\ref{tab:modes};
Fig.~\ref{fig:modes}), and mesh convergence is below 0.5\% from the
$20\times20$ mesh onward.  A uniform modal damping ratio
$\zeta=3.08\%$ is used, identified from the modal masses, stiffnesses and
damping coefficients tabulated in \citep{WangSongLiu2019}.

The identified authority factor deserves explicit discussion, because
it is large: $\kappa_a=6.8$ multiplies the first-principles
Crawley--de Luis constant so that the static top-edge deflection at
\SI{1200}{V} matches the measured $\sim\SI{0.56}{mm}$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:static}).  The reference paper does not report the patch
material or its constants; our nominal $E_p d_{31}$ assumes a plain
PZT-5H wafer, whereas the deflections reported there are consistent
with a substantially stronger effective coupling (e.g.\ a stack or
interdigitated-electrode construction, or simply larger effective
constants), which $\kappa_a$ absorbs together with bonding-layer
effects.  What matters for the conclusions is the calibrated
\emph{authority level}, anchored to measured deflections; the reported
voltage usage scales inversely with it, and the Monte-Carlo force-level
perturbations ($\pm20\%$) cover residual uncertainty.  Similarly, the
cutting coefficients $K_t, K_r$ are literature values for Al alloys
rather than identified from the reference cut, and are perturbed
$\pm20\%$ in the robustness study.

Mass-normalized modal truncation yields, for mode $k$,
\begin{equation}
\ddot\eta_k + 2\zeta_k\omega_k\dot\eta_k + \omega_k^2\eta_k
 = b_{a,k} V(t) + \phi_k\!\big(\xc(t)\big)\, F_n(t) ,
\qquad
a(t) = \sum_k \phi^{s}_k \ddot\eta_k ,
\label{eq:modal}
\end{equation}
with $b_{a,k}=\bm\Phi_k^{\!\top}\mathbf B_a$, $\phi_k(x)$ the mode shape
along the cutter path ($y_c=\SI{117}{mm}$), $\phi_k^s$ its value at the
accelerometer, and $F_n$ the plate-normal cutting force applied at the
moving position $\xc(t)$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:modal} exhibits the central
structural fact exploited in Section~\ref{sec:control}: \emph{the pair}
$(b_a,\phi^s)$ \emph{is constant; only the disturbance input row}
$\phi(\xc)$ \emph{and the performance output row}
$\phi(\xc)$ \emph{travel with the cutter}.

\subsection{Milling force with regeneration}
\label{sec:milling}

The reference process is side milling of the near-free-edge strip: a
four-flute carbide end mill (diameter \SI{12}{mm}, \SI{7000}{rpm}) with
axial band $a_p=\SI{3}{mm}$ traverses the path in \SI{10}{s}
\citep{WangSongLiu2019}.  The reference feed and radial depth
(\SI{720}{mm/min}, $a_e=\SI{0.2}{mm}$) are retained for model
validation; for the \emph{control benchmark} the severity is raised to a
declared semi-finishing condition---feed \SI{1440}{mm/min}
($f_z=\SI{51}{\micro m}$/tooth), $a_e=\SI{0.3}{mm}$, and a typical
per-tooth radial runout of \SI{2.5}{\micro m}---because with the
corrected force projection the reference condition leaves the plate
nearly quiescent (sub-micron response), whereas the reference
experiments themselves report substantial vibration and chatter marks;
their own stability diagram places their process near or beyond its
limit, indicating an effective severity that literature force
coefficients at the printed parameters do not reproduce.  At the
benchmark condition the computed critical depths
($a_{p,\mathrm{crit}}\approx$ 1.7--2.0~mm $< a_p$) put the pass in the
finite-amplitude regenerative regime with 10--20~\si{\micro m} RMS
vibration at the most compliant path region, matching the order of
magnitude observed experimentally.  With tooth angle $\varphi_j$ measured from the
surface normal and the wall deflection $w$ positive toward the tool, the
chip thickness and the plate-normal force on the workpiece are
\begin{equation}
h_j = \big[f_z\sin\varphi_j + (w(t)-w(t-\tau))\cos\varphi_j
 + \Delta r_j\big]\, g(\varphi_j),\qquad
F_n = \sum_j K_t a_p h_j\,(\sin\varphi_j - K_r\cos\varphi_j),
\label{eq:force}
\end{equation}
where the projection follows from the tool-center frame (tooth at
$R(\sin\varphi,-\cos\varphi)$, cutting velocity along
$(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$): the tangential force drags the wall
material toward the tool ($+\sin\varphi_j$) while the radial ploughing
pushes it away ($-K_r\cos\varphi_j$).  The two contributions cancel at
$\tan\varphi=K_r$ \emph{inside} typical low-immersion engagement windows,
so the net normal force and the orientation-averaged regenerative gain
$\alpha = N\big[\tfrac12\sin^2\varphi
 - K_r(\tfrac{\varphi}{2}+\tfrac{\sin 2\varphi}{4})\big]_{\varphi_{st}}
^{\varphi_{ex}}$
are both small at the reference paper's finishing condition---an
observation with a practical consequence for benchmark design discussed
below,
with $h_j\le 0$ clipped (tooth jump-out), $g$ the engagement window,
$\tau$ the tooth period, $\Delta r_j$ optional per-tooth runout, and
$K_t=\SI{796}{MPa}$, $K_r=0.212$ typical of Al alloys
\citep{Altintas2012}.  Beyond the linear stability limit the amplitude
is bounded by two physical mechanisms that a single-delay model lacks:
\emph{surface memory}---a tooth that jumps out leaves the surface of an
older pass, so the effective delay and the accumulated feed become
$m\tau$ and $m f_z$ with $m$ the consecutive missed passes
\citep{Insperger2004}---and the finite radially available material,
which caps the chip thickness.  Both are implemented in the simulator
(consecutive-miss counter per engagement window, $m\le 8$; chip cap
$a_e + m_{\max}f_z$); without them, strongly supercritical cuts diverge
unphysically instead of settling into the finite-amplitude
(``saturated'') chatter observed experimentally.  The linearized
orientation-averaged regenerative gain is negative (force opposes
penetration), reproducing classical stability behaviour: the critical
depth from the direct Nyquist criterion on
$L(\omega)= a_p (K_t\alpha/2\pi)(1-e^{-j\omega\tau})H(j\omega)$ agrees
with the growth threshold of the linearized time-domain simulation within
6\% at half immersion (Sec.~\ref{sec:results:model}).

Three features of the benchmark process shape the control problem
(Fig.~\ref{fig:force}).  First, the force is an impulsive train at
$\TPF=\SI{466.7}{Hz}$ whose fourth harmonic (\SI{1866.8}{Hz}) falls
within 0.5\% of mode~4 (\SI{1874.8}{Hz}).  Second, the runout adds
force lines at ALL spindle-frequency multiples (\SI{116.7}{Hz}
spacing), not only at the tooth-passing multiples, as in real cutting.
Third, the regenerative margin is below the axial band along the whole
path, so the uncontrolled pass operates in the finite-amplitude
(jump-out saturated) regime---any candidate controller must not degrade
this margin, and beyond it the vibration amplitude is governed by the
jump-out geometry rather than by linear damping.

\subsection{Sensing chain, actuation chain, and truth model}
\label{sec:chain}

Following the reference hardware, the accelerometer signal passes an
analog second-order Butterworth low-pass (\SI{2.5}{kHz}) before being
sampled at $f_s=\SI{10}{kHz}$; the controller output is applied through a
high-voltage amplifier saturating at $[-500, 1500]\,\si{V}$ with one
sample of computation delay.  The evaluation ("truth") model retains 20
modes (up to $\sim\SI{9.6}{kHz}$), integrated by exact per-mode
zero-order-hold propagation at \SI{50}{kHz} substeps with the nonlinear
regenerative force \eqref{eq:force} evaluated per substep from a delayed
ring buffer; the analog filter runs at the substep rate so that
above-Nyquist modes alias into the sampled measurement exactly as through
the physical chain; white measurement noise of \SI{0.05}{m/s^2} RMS is
added at the sampler.  In-process material removal is represented by a
per-element thickness reduction behind the cutter; over one pass it
shifts the modal frequencies by at most \RemovalDrift, and is included
both as a scheduled plant drift in dedicated runs and within the
robustness envelope of Sec.~\ref{sec:results:robust}.

\section{Position-scheduled harmonic LQG control}
\label{sec:control}

\subsection{Design model: composite discretization and disturbance
augmentation}
\label{sec:design-model}

The design model retains $n_m=6$ modes.  Because the analog filter
precedes the sampler, the modal equations and the filter are discretized
\emph{jointly}: with continuous state
$\mathbf x_{mf}=[\bm\eta;\dot{\bm\eta};\mathbf x_f]$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf x_{mf}[k\!+\!1] = \mathbf A_d\,\mathbf x_{mf}[k]
 + \mathbf b_V u_a[k] + \mathbf P_d\,\bm\phi(\xc)\,d[k],
\qquad y[k] = \mathbf c_y^{\!\top}\mathbf x_{mf}[k],
\label{eq:composite}
\end{equation}
where $(\mathbf A_d,\mathbf b_V,\mathbf P_d)$ is the ZOH discretization
of the composite system at $T_s=1/f_s$, $u_a$ the applied (saturated,
delayed) voltage, and $d$ the scalar cutting-force disturbance.  The
periodic force is modelled by the internal-model states
\begin{equation}
\mathbf z = [z_0;\; z_{1,1}, z_{1,2};\;\dots;\; z_{H,1}, z_{H,2}],
\qquad
\mathbf z_h[k\!+\!1] = \rho\,
\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta_h & \sin\theta_h\\
 -\sin\theta_h & \cos\theta_h\end{bmatrix}\mathbf z_h[k],
\quad \theta_h = h\,\Omega_{tp} T_s,
\label{eq:phasors}
\end{equation}
with $H=4$ harmonics and $\Omega_{tp}=2\pi\TPF$ synchronized to the
spindle speed, which is known from the CNC/encoder; a speed change
re-tunes $\theta_h$ and the associated design quantities.  (We refer to
this as spindle-\emph{synchronized} rather than adaptive: in this study
the schedule is recomputed per operating speed at design time, and the
speed-sweep experiment of Sec.~\ref{sec:results:robust} quantifies both
the synchronized and the deliberately detuned behaviour; online
adaptation during speed transients \citep{Kinney2011,Kurniawan2014} is
not claimed.)  The state $z_0$ is a \emph{broadband random-walk
force state}: with its process noise set well above that of the phasor
states, the Kalman filter uses it to track the slowly varying and
sub-harmonic force content that does not fit the harmonic model---in
particular the finite-amplitude regenerative content of supercritical
path regions, whose limit-cycle spectrum concentrates near $\TPF/m$,
at and below the first plate mode.  Its cancellation uses the static
inversion $\Lambda_0$, whose phase is reliable in the quasi-static band;
content close to mode~1 is shared between $z_0$ and the modal-state
estimates, where the LQR handles it, so the split is soft rather than
frequency-gated.  The leakage $\rho=0.99995$ replaces the exact
marginally stable internal model \citep{FrancisWonham1976} with a
contraction whose budget $(1-\rho)$ absorbs model mismatch; the induced
steady cancellation bias is $O(1\%)$, standard practice in repetitive and
adaptive-feedforward control \citep{Kurniawan2014,Bodson1997}.

Because the intersample behaviour of $d$ is sinusoidal, not
zero-order-held, the phasor states couple into \eqref{eq:composite}
through the \emph{exact} sinusoid discretization
\begin{equation}
\mathbf M_h(\xc) = \big(e^{j\theta_h}\mathbf I - \mathbf A_d\big)
\big(j h\Omega_{tp}\mathbf I - \mathbf A\big)^{-1}
\mathbf P\,\bm\phi(\xc),
\qquad
\Delta\mathbf x_{mf} = \operatorname{Re}\mathbf M_h\, z_{h,1}
 + \operatorname{Im}\mathbf M_h\, z_{h,2},
\label{eq:sincoupling}
\end{equation}
which matters at the higher harmonics where a ZOH approximation rotates
the phase by tens of degrees per sample.  The augmented state
$\hat{\mathbf x} = [\mathbf x_{mf};\mathbf z;u_{\mathrm{prev}}]$ (the
delay state completes the model) has dimension $2n_m{+}2{+}(2H{+}1){+}1=24$.

\subsection{Estimation, scheduled feedback, and scheduled harmonic
cancellation}
\label{sec:law}

A steady-state Kalman predictor is designed on a grid of 31 cutter
positions (the position enters only the disturbance coupling columns),
with process noise on the modal velocities and on the phasor states and
the measured-noise variance of the sensing chain; on-circle internal
models make the standard DARE solvers fail, so the Riccati difference
equation is iterated to stationarity.  At runtime the predictor uses the
position-continuous model matrices and gains interpolated from the grid,
and is driven by the \emph{saturated} control (anti-windup):
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf x}[k\!+\!1] = \mathbf A(\xc)\hat{\mathbf x}[k]
 + \mathbf b\,\operatorname{sat}(u[k])
 + \mathbf L(\xc)\big(y[k] - \mathbf c^{\!\top}\hat{\mathbf x}[k]\big).
\label{eq:kf}
\end{equation}
Note that the output map $\mathbf c$ is \emph{constant} (the
accelerometer is fixed---the structural fact of
Sec.~\ref{sec:plate}); the position enters only through the disturbance
coupling columns of $\mathbf A(\xc)$ and through the scheduled gains.

The control combines a position-scheduled LQR on the controllable
subsystem $[\mathbf x_{mf};u_{\mathrm{prev}}]$ with cost
\begin{equation}
J = \sum_k \big(\bm\phi(\xc[k])^{\!\top}\hat{\bm\eta}[k]\big)^2
 + r_u\, u[k]^2 ,
\label{eq:lqr}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the deflection at the \emph{moving cutting point} is weighted---the
rigorous form of the idea approximated by heuristic gain scheduling in
\citep{WangSongLiu2019}---and a disturbance-accommodating harmonic
cancellation \citep{Johnson1971}: with
$G^K_u(z)$ and $G^K_{d,h}(z)$ the design-model transfers from the
(delayed) control and from harmonic $h$ to the cutting-point deflection
evaluated \emph{with the LQR loop closed} (the state feedback also
responds to the disturbance-driven states, so inverting the open-loop
ratio would leave a systematic residual), the cancelling phasor gain is
the regularized inversion
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_h(\xc) = \frac{G^K_{d,h}\,\overline{G^K_u}}
 {|G^K_u|^2+\varepsilon_h^2},\qquad
\varepsilon_h = \epsilon_r \max_{x\in\text{path}}\big|G^K_u(e^{j\theta_h};x)\big|,
\label{eq:ffgain}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
u_{\mathrm{ff}} = -\operatorname{Re}\Lambda_0\, \hat z_0
 - \sum_{h=1}^{H}\big(\operatorname{Re}\Lambda_h\,\hat z_{h,1}
 + \operatorname{Im}\Lambda_h\,\hat z_{h,2}\big),
\label{eq:ff}
\end{equation}
all evaluated at $z_h=e^{j\theta_h}$ and scheduled on $\xc$.  The
regularization floor $\varepsilon_h$ is \emph{absolute} per harmonic
(a fraction $\epsilon_r=0.05$ of the path-maximum authority), so the
inversion gain is genuinely bounded at positions where the local
authority collapses---e.g.\ the second harmonic near mid-path, which
falls between modes~2 and~3 while mode~2 decouples from the
$x$-symmetric patch.  The total
command is $u = -\mathbf K(\xc)\,[\hat{\mathbf x}_{mf};
\hat u_{\mathrm{prev}}] + u_{\mathrm{ff}}$, saturated by the amplifier.
The LQR weight $r_u$ is auto-tuned: a bisection finds the most aggressive
weight whose frozen closed loop against the \emph{full 20-mode} model
keeps all structural eigenvalues below $0.997$ and all internal-model
eigenvalues within half the leakage budget along the whole path, backed
off by a factor of 3.

\subsection{Spillover verification and masking}
\label{sec:spillover}

The inversion \eqref{eq:ff} is computed on the reduced model; at
positions where the actuation authority at a harmonic is carried by a
small difference of modal tails (e.g.\ the second harmonic near the path
midpoint, which falls between modes~2 and~3 while mode~2 decouples from
the $x$-symmetric patch), residual-mode truncation can flip its phase and
push the corresponding internal-model pole outside the unit circle---a
harmonic-specific observation/control spillover \citep{Balas1978}.  A
verification loop against the full-order model therefore checks the
frozen poles in a $\pm\SI{30}{Hz}$ band around each harmonic along the
path and locally backs off the offending $\Lambda_h$ until the whole
path verifies.  One ambiguity deserves explicit treatment: a
\emph{plate} pole can fall inside a harmonic band (here mode~4 at
\SI{1874.8}{Hz} vs.\ the 4th harmonic at \SI{1866.8}{Hz}), and no
frequency criterion can distinguish it from an internal-model pole.
The design resolves this by attribution-through-damping: backing off
$\Lambda_h$ can only stabilize poles that belong to the internal-model
mechanism, so a structural pole destabilized inside a band fails the
masking loop and forces the $r_u$ auto-tuner to a weaker feedback---
in-band poles of either origin end up subject to the same explicit
budget $|\lambda| \le 1-(1-\rho)/2$, and the strict structural margin
(0.997) is guaranteed outside the bands.  The estimation-side
counterpart of the weak-authority problem (reduced observability of a
harmonic's phasor when $\bm\phi(\xc)$ nearly annihilates the modes
carrying it) is handled by the same masking, which attenuates exactly
those position--harmonic pairs.

\subsection{Stability argument}
\label{sec:stability}

Three complementary layers support closed-loop stability claims:
(i)~\emph{frozen-position analysis}: the exact discrete closed loop
(20-mode composite plant, delay, filter, full controller) has spectral
radius $\le 0.99995$ at every point of a dense path grid
(Fig.~\ref{fig:frozen}), the bound being attained by the in-band pole
cluster at its explicit budget $1-(1-\rho)/2$;
(ii)~\emph{slow variation, stated with its limits}: the schedule
traverses the path in \SI{10}{s}, i.e.\ one grid cell per $\sim$3300
control samples.  For the structural dynamics (decay times of
milliseconds to tens of milliseconds) this is orders of magnitude slower
than the loop, and frozen-point conclusions carry over by standard
slowly-varying-systems arguments.  The internal-model pole cluster,
however, is deliberately slow (leakage time constant
$T_s/(1-\rho)\approx\SI{2}{s}$, the same order as the traversal), so the
frozen argument is NOT invoked for it; its stability rests on the
uniform contraction budget $1-\rho$ enforced along the entire path by
the design criterion, which holds for arbitrary parameter rates;
(iii)~\emph{a posteriori verification}: all reported closed-loop passes,
including the Monte-Carlo perturbations, are full nonlinear time-domain
simulations of the time-varying loop with the regenerative delay closed;
no run exhibits divergence or persistent saturation.  The regenerative
loop itself is assessed by the stability-lobe analysis of
Sec.~\ref{sec:results:sld} using the frozen \emph{closed-loop} FRFs.

\section{Baseline controllers}
\label{sec:baselines}

\textbf{CPD.}  A constant PD on the filtered acceleration,
$u=-(k_p y + k_d\Delta y)$.  Tuning is three-staged and fully disclosed:
a signed log-grid of candidates is pre-filtered by (a)~frozen stability
and (b)~non-degradation of the regenerative margin
($a_{\mathrm{crit}}^{\mathrm{cl}} \ge
\min(0.9\,a_{\mathrm{crit}}^{\mathrm{ol}},\,1.3\,a_p)$), ranked by a
frequency-domain surrogate (response to the rigid-force harmonics with
a soft actuator-voltage budget and a broadband waterbed guard
$\le 1.2\times$ the open-loop FRF peak), and the leading candidates are
then \emph{scored by nonlinear time-domain simulation} at three path
positions---necessary because in the finite-amplitude regenerative
regime the linear surrogates rank candidates incorrectly.  The winner
is verified once more by simulation before use.  This gives the constant
law of \citep{WangSongLiu2019} a considerably stronger tuning than in
the original work.

\textbf{VPD.}  The published time-space varying PD, re-derived
simulation-truthfully: the CPD winner anchors the gain shape, a
position-dependent intensity $s(x)\in[0.5,2.2]$ is selected at each of
31 grid points by short nonlinear simulations at that point, and the
resulting profiles are smoothed by the quintic-polynomial
parameterization of \citep{WangSongLiu2019} (their Eq.~26).  We found
that the smoothing step is not benign: the quintic can bridge over
local dips of the point-wise stabilizing gains and destabilize the
loop---an effect that frozen eigenvalue analysis confirms and that
frequency-domain chatter checks alone can miss near phase-sensitive
stability pockets of the interrupted-cutting regenerative loop.  The
baseline therefore verifies the fitted profile (frozen, regenerative,
and nonlinear-simulation checks) and shrinks it until every check
passes, erroring out rather than returning an unverified profile.
This safeguard is itself one of the paper's findings: the published VPD
parameterization requires re-verification after smoothing.

\subsection{Milling-pass comparison}

The benchmark pass operates beyond the linear regenerative limit along
the whole path, in the finite-amplitude (jump-out) regime.  The
uncontrolled response concentrates where the regenerative margin is
lowest and the plate most compliant (around mid-path), with a mixed
spectrum of spindle/TPF force lines and sub-harmonic limit-cycle
content near $\TPF/m$; toward the path ends the response falls to the
micron scale.  This structure governs what any controller of the
configuration can achieve: at the hot region PSH-LQG suppresses the
response by close to an order of magnitude with a few tens of volts
(harmonic lines down 20--40~dB, Fig.~\ref{fig:volt} right), while at
the near-quiescent path regions all controllers are limited by the
signal scale itself.  Over the whole pass, PSH-LQG reduces the
cutting-point AC RMS by \RedPSH\ (vs.\ \RedCPD\ for CPD and \RedVPD\
for VPD), with moderate voltage (RMS \VrmsPSH, no saturation;
Fig.~\ref{fig:volt} left), and reduces the surface-proxy standard
deviation by \SurfPSH; CPD and VPD, honestly tuned and safeguarded, are
close to each other (the published advantage of VPD over CPD stemmed
largely from a weakly tuned constant baseline), and their best action
is derivative (direct damping), consistent with the
regeneration-dominated physics.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:results:robust}

Twenty Monte-Carlo passes perturb simultaneously the truth-model
frequencies ($\pm3\%$, covering both identification error and the
material-removal drift), damping ($\pm30\%$), force level ($\pm20\%$),
per-tooth runout (0--\SI{5}{\micro m}), and the computation delay
(doubled in 30\% of runs), with all three controllers kept at their
nominal designs.  Figure~\ref{fig:robust} shows the distributions of
RMS reduction factors: PSH-LQG retains a median reduction of
\MCmedPSH\ (interquartile range \MCiqrPSH, worst case \MCminPSH),
degrading gracefully, and never destabilizes nor saturates
persistently; the sample size (20) supports order statistics, not
tail-probability claims, which we do not make.  A dedicated pass
against the drifting-frequency plant (scheduled material removal)
matches the nominal performance within \RemovalPerf.  Under
spindle-speed changes (Fig.~\ref{fig:rpm}), where the open-loop
response changes strongly as the spindle-harmonic comb moves relative
to the plate resonances, the spindle-synchronized controller retains
full performance (\RpmSixty\ at 6000~rpm, \RpmEighty\ at 8000~rpm).
Notably, a controller with the internal model left detuned at the
7000-rpm TPF degrades only mildly (by \DetunedRange): the scheduled LQR
and the broadband force state carry most of the benefit, and the
spindle-synchronous phasors provide the final refinement.  The
architecture is thus robust to TPF misinformation rather than reliant
on it---addressing the frequency-mismatch caveat of \citep{Kinney2011}
by construction.

\subsection{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:results:disc}

The comparison isolates \emph{where} the advantage of PSH-LQG comes
from: (i)~the Kalman predictor turns the single non-collocated
accelerometer into an estimate of the full modal state \emph{and} of the
force content, where PD acts on the raw local signal;
(ii)~the internal model concentrates actuation authority exactly on the
force lines that dominate the surface error, where PD spends authority
broadband; (iii)~the scheduled weights aim that authority at the moving
cutting point, which is the rigorous version of the VPD intuition.
Equally important is where the advantage \emph{ends}: in path regions cut
far beyond the linear regenerative limit, the vibration amplitude is a
geometric property of the jump-out limit cycle (of the order of the feed
per tooth) that no controller of this actuator/sensor configuration can
substantially lower---the honest engineering conclusion is that active
control of the present kind complements, and does not replace,
process-parameter selection in those regions.  The failed-variant record
is also instructive: over-widened estimator bandwidths (large
$q_{\mathrm{modal}}$ or $q_{\mathrm{dc}}$) are immediately flagged by
the frozen-stability map before any simulation is run, illustrating the
value of keeping the full-order verification inside the design loop.  The
architecture inherits LQG's lack of guaranteed margins; that is mitigated
here operationally (auto-tuned $r_u$ against the full-order model,
spillover masking, leakage budget) and verified a posteriori, but a
scheduled $H_\infty$/LMI synthesis with certified parameter-rate bounds
is a natural extension.  The study is simulation-based; the simulator is
deliberately harsher than the design model (order, nonlinearity, noise,
saturation, delay, drift), and every hardware parameter is anchored to
the published experiment, yet cutting tests remain the necessary next
step.  Piezo hysteresis and amplifier bandwidth are not modelled
\citep{Sun2025Review}; the scheduling map is offline-FEM-derived, and
coupling it to online model updating \citep{Shi2023,TuysuzAltintas2017}
is a promising direction.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:concl}

A position-scheduled harmonic LQG architecture was developed for active
vibration suppression in thin-walled plate milling with a fixed
piezoelectric patch and a single fixed accelerometer.  The formulation
recognizes that the plant is LTI while the disturbance input and the
performance output travel with the cutter, and therefore schedules the
Kalman disturbance coupling, the LQR performance weight, and a
regularized harmonic-cancellation inversion on the cutter position, with
a spindle-synchronized internal model of the tooth-passing force
harmonics complemented by a broadband force state.
Implementation realism (saturation with anti-windup, computation delay,
analog anti-aliasing, spillover masking against the full-order model) is
part of the design, and the evaluation closes the nonlinear regenerative
loop on a 20-mode truth model.  Against honestly tuned PD baselines---
including a faithful, safeguarded re-derivation of the published VPD
strategy, whose quintic gain smoothing is shown to require post-fit
stability verification---the proposed controller delivers an
order-of-magnitude, nearly position-uniform reduction of the
cutting-point vibration, enlarges the predicted stability envelope where
the process is marginal, and is robust to plant, force, delay, runout,
spindle-speed and material-removal perturbations.  The complete model,
controllers, and campaign are released as an open repository to serve as
a reproducible benchmark for time-space varying machining control.

$d(t)=\operatorname{Re}\{a_k e^{j\omega (t-kT_s)}\}$ on

\[
\mathbf x[k\!+\!1] = e^{\mathbf A T_s}\mathbf x[k]
 + \operatorname{Re}\big\{\big(e^{j\omega T_s}\mathbf I -

\]
